## Welcome

Microsoft 365 Copilot is built into the apps you already use every day — Outlook, Word, and Excel. It can save you real time on drafting, summarising, and number-crunching. But like any tool, you get much better results when you know how to use it well.

This guide skips the theory. It gives you a simple formula to remember, then shows you real prompts you can copy, adapt, and use today.

## The Good Prompt Formula

A prompt that works well usually answers four questions:

> **Who am I? → What do I want? → What should it look like? → Any important detail?**

You do not need all four every time, but the more you include, the better the output.

**Easy version to remember:**

> **My role** — **the task** — **what it should look like** — **any key detail**

**Example in one sentence:**

> *"I'm a finance administrator. Draft a short email to a supplier apologising for a delayed payment. Keep it professional and under 100 words. The payment was due last Friday."*

That is it. Role → Task → Format → Detail. Keep this in your head and you will write good prompts every time.

## Outlook — Email Drafting and Summarising

### Example 1 — Drafting a reply you are dreading

> *"Draft a polite but firm reply to this email. The sender is asking us to approve an invoice we cannot process yet because we are waiting for a purchase order number. Ask them to resend with the PO number included and thank them for their patience."*

**Why it works:** You have told Copilot the situation, the action needed, and the tone.

---

### Example 2 — Summarising a long email thread

> *"Summarise this email thread in 5 bullet points. I need to know: what the request is, who is responsible for the next action, and what the deadline is."*

**Why it works:** You are telling it exactly what you need from the summary, not just asking it to "summarise".

---

### Example 3 — Writing a meeting follow-up

> *"Write a follow-up email to the team after today's budget review meeting. Include: decisions made are still to be confirmed, actions go to Sarah and Tom, and the next meeting is Thursday at 2pm. Friendly but professional tone."*

**Why it works:** You have given it the key facts. Copilot fills in the structure and wording — you supply the content.

## Word — Drafting and Editing Documents

### Example 1 — Turning bullet points into a proper paragraph

> *"I have written these rough notes from a client call. Turn them into a clear, professional paragraph suitable for an internal briefing note. Keep it factual and under 150 words."*

*(Then paste your bullet points below the prompt.)*

**Why it works:** You are giving Copilot raw material and clear instructions on what to do with it.

---

### Example 2 — Rewriting something for a different audience

> *"Rewrite the following section so it is easy to understand for someone who doesn't work in finance. Use short sentences, avoid technical terms, and write it as if you're explaining it to a friend."*

*(Then paste the section you want rewritten.)*

**Why it works:** Telling Copilot who will be reading it makes a huge difference. The more specific you are about your audience, the more useful the result.

---

### Example 3 — Creating a first draft of a standard document

> *"Write a one-page policy summary explaining our expense claim process for new starters. Include: what counts as a claimable expense, how to submit a claim, and the approval timeline. Friendly, clear tone."*

**Why it works:** You have defined the document type, the audience, the structure, and the tone. Copilot can produce a solid first draft you then edit — rather than you staring at a blank page.

## Excel — Analysis and Formulas

### Example 1 — Explaining what a formula does

> *"Explain what this formula does in plain English, as if you are explaining it to someone who has never used Excel: =SUMIFS(D2:D100,B2:B100,"London",C2:C100,"Q1")"*

> **Tip:** Not sure how to find your formula? Click on the cell that contains it — the full formula appears in the bar along the top of your spreadsheet. Copy that and paste it at the end of your prompt.

**Why it works:** Copilot is excellent at translating formulas into plain language. Really useful when you are trying to make sense of a spreadsheet someone else built.

---

### Example 2 — Writing a formula from a description

> *"Write an Excel formula that adds up all the numbers in column B, but only where the word in column A says Approved. My data starts on row 2."*

**Why it works:** You do not need to know the name of the formula or how to write it — just describe what you want it to do. Copilot works out the rest.

---

### Example 3 — Summarising data insights

> *"I have a spreadsheet showing monthly sales figures by region for the past 12 months. Based on this data, give me a three-sentence plain-English summary of the key trends — which regions are growing, which are declining, and whether there is a seasonal pattern."*

**Why it works:** Instead of spending 20 minutes building a chart and writing commentary, you get a starting point in seconds that you can verify and refine.

## A Note on Keeping Data Safe

The good news first: Microsoft 365 Copilot is part of your FinBridge work environment. Your prompts stay inside FinBridge's systems — they do not go off to a public website. So for everyday work tasks, you are already in the right place.

The one habit to carry everywhere is this:

> **Before you type a prompt, check it does not contain real names, client details, or anything confidential.**

This matters most if you ever use a free public AI tool (like a chatbot on a website) outside of your work apps. Those tools are not connected to FinBridge's systems, so anything you type into them leaves your organisation.

A simple check before you press send:
- Could someone outside FinBridge identify a real person or client from what I have typed?
- Does it include figures or information I would not share outside the organisation?

If the answer to either is yes, just swap in a stand-in word: write `[client name]` instead of the real name, or `[amount]` instead of the real figure. You get just as useful a response — without any risk.

This is the same habit from **Day 1 of this programme**. It takes two seconds and quickly becomes automatic.

## If a Prompt Does Not Work First Time

- **Add more detail.** The more context you give, the more useful the answer.
- **Tell it the audience.** "Write for a new starter" gives a different result than "write for the finance director."
- **Tell it the format.** "Give me 5 bullet points" or "write two short paragraphs."
- **Break it up.** If the task is complex, do it in two or three prompts rather than one long one.

The more you practise, the more natural it becomes. Start with one of the examples above and adapt it to something you actually need today.
